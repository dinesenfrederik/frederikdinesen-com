*The Dimming Effect* is Rise Upon's 2024 album. I play drums and write music in the band, and on the album I was also the producer, recording engineer and mixing engineer. The album was nominated for a GAFFA Award in the Danish hard rock/metal category, and the band was nominated for the audience award at Den Hårde Tone, the Danish rock and metal awards.

That might sound like a big studio production. It wasn't. The album was recorded at four different locations, most of them private home studios, and a large part of the guitar and bass work was done with a laptop, an audio interface and amp sims. In this case study I walk through how we did it, what was hard, and what I would do differently today.

In short, *The Dimming Effect* was recorded at four locations. The drums were tracked acoustically in a studio at our rehearsal-space association, guitar and bass in Drop A with amp sims, mainly from Neural DSP, without re-amping, and vocals and synths in my home studio in Aarhus, Denmark. I mixed the album in Studio One, and Jacob Hansen at Hansen Studios mastered it.

## Four locations and a laptop

A full metal album can be recorded today without booking a big studio for weeks. *The Dimming Effect* was recorded in four places: the drums in a studio at our rehearsal-space association, part of the guitar and bass tracks at our guitarist's place and with family members, and vocals and synths in my home studio in Aarhus, where I run FD Sound Labs today.

**The advantage of recording simply is that the recording can happen where the musicians are.** With a laptop, an audio interface and good amp sims, the guitarist could record whenever there was time and energy, and we weren't tied to expensive studio days.

The downside is that you have to keep track of everything. When tracks come from four places, sessions, tempo, naming and file formats have to match, or you end up spending your mixing time cleaning up.

---

## Drums: a dead room and creative mic placement

The drums were recorded acoustically in the studio at our rehearsal-space association. The room was very dead, with almost no natural reverb. That gives you control, but it can also make drums sound small and dry.

So the focus was on two things:

* **Good takes and good drum sounds from the start.** In a dead room there's no reverb to hide behind, so head tuning and tight playing are everything.
* **Creative room mic placement.** Besides the close mics, I placed room mics in unconventional spots to get as much body out of the drums as possible.

In the mix I blended the acoustic drums with samples from various producers, including my own. Sample blending (layering samples with the recorded drums) gave the punch and consistency a modern metal production needs, while the acoustic tracks kept the dynamics of the performance. Everything was mixed and sampled in Studio One.

## Guitar and bass in Drop A with amp sims

All guitar and bass on the album were recorded in Drop A with amp sims, mainly from Neural DSP, combined with other creative plugin chains. We didn't use re-amping.

That means the tone was chosen during recording, not afterwards. **The advantage is that the musician plays into the sound the song needs**, and fewer decisions are left for the mix. The downside is that you can't swap the amp later if the tone doesn't sit in the final mix.

> **Pro tip:** If you record with amp sims, always save a clean DI track alongside, even if you don't plan to re-amp. It costs nothing and gives you a safety net if the tone needs to change in the mix.

In Drop A the guitars' lowest fundamental sits around 55 Hz, right in the kick drum and bass territory. That puts a lot of pressure on the low-end balance. I've written more about handling that in my guide to [fixing a muddy low end in a metal mix](/en/blog/fix-muddy-low-end-metal-mix).

## Vocals: many layers of clean vocals

The vocals were recorded in my home studio in Aarhus. The album has only a few screams and growls, so the focus was on sharp clean takes.

* **Many layers:** We recorded many vocal layers and doubles to give the vocals a wide, big sound that could stand next to references like Bad Omens and Starset.
* **Heavy compression and EQ:** In the mix I used classic methods with heavy compression and EQ, so the vocal sits steadily and cuts through the heavy, downtuned guitars.

**The most important thing for a clean vocal to carry a heavy mix is a steady, well-recorded take.** Compression and EQ can shape the sound, but they can't rescue an unsure performance.

## The mix: perfectionism and starting over

The biggest challenge on *The Dimming Effect* wasn't technical. It was that I was mixing my own music.

When you've written, played and recorded the songs yourself, it's hard to hear them objectively. I got perfectionist and a bit unfocused: I did too much and still didn't quite hit the sounds I had in my head. After mixing the first few songs, I decided to start over.

The second time I mixed with fresh ears and didn't touch everything that was already well recorded. The result was far better. Two things helped most:

* **Input from the band.** Four of us made the music, and when I asked the others for their take on a sound or a performance, they often brought an angle that got it over the line.
* **Fresh ears.** Breaks and distance made it easier to hear what the mix actually needed, rather than what I thought it needed.

---

## Mastering by Jacob Hansen

The album was mastered by Jacob Hansen at Hansen Studios. The mastering made the mix wider, a bit heavier and gave the final polish to a mix I had worked hard on.

That's also why I recommend bands get their music mastered by a dedicated mastering engineer today. An extra set of fresh ears on the finished mix lifts the release, especially when you've been close to the whole process yourself.

## What I would do differently today

If I made *The Dimming Effect* again, these are the most important lessons. They apply to most bands who record themselves:

1. **Spend more time on the recording.** Good takes and good sounds from the start don't need as much polishing in the mix. Every hour spent getting a take right saves several hours later.
2. **Remember that less is more in the mix.** Well-recorded tracks need fewer moves, not more. When you start tweaking for the sake of tweaking, it's time for a break.
3. **Keep tracks from several locations consistent.** The same tempo, naming and file format from every location makes the mix faster and better.
4. **Get fresh ears.** Ask your bandmates, or let someone else listen, when you're stuck in your own material.
